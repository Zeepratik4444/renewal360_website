A renewal process is quietly broken when renewals still close most of the time, but nobody could say why the losses happened until afterwards. The seven warning signs: renewal knowledge lives in one person's head, at-risk accounts surface 30 days out or later, every account gets the same outreach, churn surprises you, CSMs spend hours writing renewal emails, leaders cannot see revenue at risk without a report, and automated follow-ups fire after the customer has already replied.

Broken renewal processes rarely look broken. The renewal rate is acceptable, the team is busy, and the spreadsheet is up to date most weeks. The cost hides in the renewals you lose late, the expansions you never raise, and the hours spent on admin. Here is how to spot it.

Sign 1: Renewal knowledge lives in one person's head

If one CSM or team lead is the only person who knows which renewals are coming and which are shaky, you have a single point of failure. Holidays, a resignation or a busy quarter turn into missed renewals.

The fix: keep the renewal book, and each renewal's status and gaps, in a shared system everyone can see.

Sign 2: You find out about at-risk accounts 30 days out or later

By the last month, the customer has usually decided. If most of your saves start in the final weeks, you are reacting to signals that appeared months earlier: a champion going quiet, usage falling, support issues piling up.

The fix: watch engagement, people, usage and support for every account, all year, and start the renewal conversation 90 to 120 days out for annual contracts.

Sign 3: Every account gets the same outreach

The same three emails at 60, 30 and 7 days for a $5,000 account and a $200,000 account means you are under-serving your largest customers and over-contacting your smallest.

The fix: segment by size, contract type and risk, and give each segment its own steps. See how to build a renewal playbook.

Sign 4: Churn always surprises you in the post-mortem

If churn reviews keep finding warning signs nobody acted on, the information existed but nobody saw it in time. It was in an inbox, a ticket or a usage chart.

The fix: read those sources together for every account, and write down the reasons an account is at risk, not just a score.

Sign 5: CSMs spend hours a week writing renewal emails

Check-ins, who-signs questions, reminders and thank-you notes are similar every time. Writing them from scratch is time not spent on the accounts that need a real conversation.

The fix: have routine emails drafted for each account, with a person approving them, and let the most routine ones run on their own once trusted. More on this in how to automate SaaS renewal emails.

Sign 6: Leaders cannot answer "how much revenue is at risk this quarter?" without a report

If the answer needs someone to build a spreadsheet the night before, it is already out of date, and it is usually built from CSM gut feel rather than evidence.

The fix: a live view of renewals by risk, accounts that got worse this week, and the most common gaps across the book.

Sign 7: Automated follow-ups fire after the customer has already replied

Nothing says "nobody is paying attention" like a cheerful reminder arriving the day after a customer raised a complaint. Sequences that do not read replies are worse than no automation.

The fix: any automation must stop when the customer replies, and hand the account back to a person.

How many of these apply to your team?

  • One or two: normal growing pains. Fix them with process: a shared renewal book and a simple playbook.
  • Three or four: you are likely losing renewals you could have saved. It is worth putting a system in place this quarter.
  • Five or more: renewals depend on luck and effort. Start with visibility of risk, then automate the routine work.

Frequently asked questions

What is the earliest sign a SaaS customer may not renew?

Usually a drop in engagement: slower replies, cancelled meetings, or the champion going quiet. Falling product usage and open support issues often follow.

How early should we know an account is at risk?

For annual contracts, ideally three to four months before the renewal date, early enough to run a business review and address problems before the commercial conversation.

Can a spreadsheet fix a broken renewal process?

It can fix signs 1 and 6 for a small book if someone keeps it current. It cannot read emails, tickets or usage, so signs 2, 4 and 7 usually remain.
