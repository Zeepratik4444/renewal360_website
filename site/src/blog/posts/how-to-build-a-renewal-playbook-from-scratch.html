A renewal playbook is the written set of steps your team takes before every renewal: who does what, when, and for which accounts. To build one from scratch, define your renewal segments, decide which signals mean an account needs help, map the steps against the renewal date, plan what happens when an account is at risk, and decide what leaders need to see. This guide walks through each step.

A playbook is not a folder of email templates. It is a set of decisions made once, so every CSM handles renewals the way your best CSM would, and nothing depends on someone remembering. Done well, it also becomes the rulebook for automation: once the steps are written down, software can run the routine ones for you.

Step 1: Define your renewal segments

One sequence for every account is the most common mistake. A $5,000 self-serve customer and a $200,000 enterprise customer need different handling. Start with two or three dimensions:

  • Size: split accounts into two or three tiers by contract value. Larger accounts get earlier starts, more people involved and more human touch.
  • Contract type: auto-renewing contracts behave differently from ones that need a signature. For auto-renewals, the moment that matters is the notice window, not the end date.
  • Risk: healthy, at risk, or in trouble. The same account can move between these during the year.

Questions to answer: Where is the line between your tiers? Which accounts renew automatically? Who owns each tier?

Step 2: Decide which signals mean an account needs help

Pick a small set of signals you can actually see, and write down what each one means for your business:

  • Engagement: how recently the customer replied or met with you.
  • People: whether your champion is still there and still answering, and whether you know anyone else.
  • Usage: whether product use is rising, flat or falling compared with a few months ago.
  • Support: serious tickets open close to the renewal.
  • Sentiment: the tone of recent replies, and any survey feedback.

Then add the readiness questions every renewal must answer before the date: Are they getting value? Is the champion engaged? Do we know more than one person? Do we know who signs? Is anything blocking it? Has pricing been discussed? Do we know how the renewal process works? Have we held a real business review recently? A signal tells you to look. An unanswered readiness question tells you what to do.

Step 3: Map the steps against the renewal date

Lay out the steps for each segment, counting back from the renewal date (or the notice date for auto-renewals). A typical shape for an annual contract:

WhenStepPurpose
About 120 days outInternal review of the accountCheck value, people, usage and open issues while there is time to act
About 90 days outBusiness review or value check-inShow results and agree goals for next year
About 75 days outConfirm who signs and how renewal worksAvoid procurement surprises at the end
About 60 days outCommercial conversationDiscuss pricing and terms before anything is sent
About 30 days outPaperwork and final reminderMake signing easy
After renewalThank-you and next-year planStart the next cycle on a good note

Large accounts start earlier; small ones can compress the middle steps. Two rules make any timeline work. First, every step stops when the customer replies, and the owner decides what comes next. Second, never ask the same question twice: if the customer already told you who signs, skip that step.

For each step, also decide how it runs: the owner does it by hand, the system drafts it for approval, or, for routine steps that have proven themselves, the system sends it on its own.

Step 4: Plan what happens when an account is at risk

Write the escalation track before you need it. When an account turns risky, the routine sequence is not enough. Decide in advance:

  • Who gets told: the owner, their manager, and at what risk level.
  • What changes: routine emails pause; the owner calls; a save plan starts.
  • When to bring in executives: for your largest accounts, an executive introduction often does more than another email.
  • What "no answer" means: after a set number of unanswered attempts, try a different contact or channel rather than repeating yourself.

Step 5: Decide what leaders need to see

A playbook that leaders cannot see is a playbook nobody checks. Agree on a short weekly view:

  • Revenue renewing this quarter, split by risk.
  • Accounts that got worse since last week, and why.
  • The most common gaps across the book, such as no signer known or no recent business review, sorted by money at stake.
  • Accounts that have gone quiet.

This view turns coaching from "how are your renewals?" into "these three accounts have no known signer; what is the plan?"

What a complete playbook looks like on one page

  • Segments: tiers by size, auto-renewing contracts handled separately, risk as a live overlay.
  • Signals and readiness questions: what you watch, and what every renewal must answer.
  • Steps: timed against the renewal or notice date, each with an owner and a way of running.
  • Escalation: who is told, what pauses, when executives step in.
  • Leadership view: the weekly picture and the gaps to coach on.

Frequently asked questions

How far before the renewal should a playbook start?

For annual contracts, about 90 to 120 days before the renewal date, earlier for large or complex accounts. For auto-renewing contracts, count back from the notice deadline instead of the end date.

How many steps should a renewal playbook have?

Fewer than you think. Five to eight steps per segment is enough for most teams. Every step should have a clear purpose; drop any step that only exists to send another email.

Should every account get the same playbook?

No. Segment by size and contract type at least. Large accounts need earlier starts and more human involvement; small accounts can run more steps automatically.

Which playbook steps can be automated safely?

Routine, low-stakes steps such as check-ins, notice-window reminders and thank-you notes, once they have been approved as written many times. Keep pricing, contract wording and your largest accounts with a person.
